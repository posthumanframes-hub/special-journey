Notes on this site

Why Athangudi tiles

The tiles on this site are drawn from the hand-made cement floors of Athangudi, a village in the Chettinad region of Tamil Nadu.

A short history

In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, Nattukottai Chettiar merchants of Chettinad traded and lent money across Burma, Ceylon, Malaya and Vietnam.1 With that wealth they built large mansions at home, using materials from across their trading world: teak from Burma, and tiles from places such as Japan, Germany, France and England.2 The village clusters of Chettinad are now on India’s UNESCO Tentative List.3

Athangudi’s hand-made tiles grew alongside these houses. Accounts of their origin differ; one study suggests local makers may have been influenced by European tiles and glazed Chinese ware.4 The method is unusual: the tiles are not fired. Coloured cement made with oxide pigments is poured into a stencil laid on a glass plate, backed with cement and sand, then cured in water. The glass gives the face its sheen.45 Much of the village is still involved in the work.6

A floor as a system

The term socio-technical system comes from studies of coal mining in the 1950s, which found that the technology of work and the social organisation around it shape each other and cannot be redesigned separately.7 An Athangudi floor fits that idea. It depends on a material and a hand technique, on families who hold the skill, and on patrons who want the floor. The pressures on it are social as much as technical: makers worry that fewer artisans want to carry the work on,8 and the craft has no heritage designation or GI tag to protect it.4 Its recent revival has come from people choosing it again for new homes.8

That is the lens I bring to my own work: a technology is never only the object, but the people, materials, money and habits that hold it in place.
